# Lesson

## Observation

Three recurring patterns in quantitative business cases:

1. **Row-count inflation:** Summing workstream classification rows instead of counting unique Action IDs. When actions span multiple workstreams, row sums exceed unique counts. This produces a number that looks precise but is actually a sum of classifications, not items.

2. **Uniform time assumption:** Multiplying action count by a fixed hours/week figure (e.g., 37 × 2 = 74 hours). This assumes every action consumes the same time every week. In reality: some actions are dormant between dependencies, some need 5 minutes of chasing, some need several days. The uniform assumption overstates by a factor of 3-5x.

3. **Revenue-as-payback:** Comparing top-line revenue to cost as if revenue = profit. "One RM168K sale pays for a RM120K salary" is a revenue comparison, not an economic break-even. The correct comparison is RM168K × contribution margin vs salary cost.

## Pattern

All three patterns share a common root: **arithmetically tempting, operationally indefensible.** The math is correct; the assumptions are not. A fact-checker who examines the assumptions (not just the arithmetic) will always find the weakness.

## Corrective Action

- **Count by unique ID, not row.** If items appear in multiple categories, the defensible number is the unique count, not the row sum.
- **Do not assume uniform time per item.** Only use time estimates with actual tracking data. If no tracking exists, say so.
- **Use contribution margin for ROI.** Revenue is top-line. Payback requires margin. If margin is unknown, state the comparison as "top-line revenue" not "pays for itself."

## Application

This lesson should be applied to any future CognitiveOS output that makes quantitative claims about capacity, workload, or ROI. The fact-check pattern (verify arithmetic, then verify assumptions, then verify framing) is the standard.

## 1. Audience Taxonomy

Every person DAF communicates with falls into one of five audience types. Each type reads differently, decides differently, and needs a different artifact.

### Type 1: Executive Authority
**Who:** CEO, COO, CFO, DG, KSU, Minister-level  
**Examples:** Aras C-suite, PMO officials (Khairil Hilmi, Dasrafeezal), LHDN leadership  
**What they need:** Strategic value, risk, cost, decision request — in that order  
**Attention span:** 5 minutes. One page.  
**Tone:** Formal, concise, outcome-oriented  
**They say yes when:** The ask is clear, the risk is bounded, the return is quantified  

### Type 2: Technical Peer
**Who:** CTO, tech leads, security architects, engineering managers  
**Examples:** Hadri (Aras), Hafeez (ELSA), internal tech team  
**What they need:** Architecture, feasibility, integration points, dependencies  
**Attention span:** 30 minutes. Up to 10 pages if technical depth is real.  
**Tone:** Peer-to-peer, precise, evidence-based  
**They say yes when:** The technical path is credible and they can see their role in it  

### Type 3: Institutional Partner
**Who:** Academics, government directors, agency heads, university centres  
**Examples:** Prof. Suhaimee (UiTM), CSM leadership, NACSA directors  
**What they need:** Shared value, governance model, legitimacy, mutual benefit  
**Attention span:** 15 minutes. 2-3 pages.  
**Tone:** Respectful, collaborative, institution-preserving  
**They say yes when:** Their institution gains capability or recognition without losing autonomy  

### Type 4: Commercial Counterpart
**Who:** Business partners, vendors, JV partners, clients  
**Examples:** ELSA commercial team, MTAI (Samantha, Farul), potential clients  
**What they need:** Commercial terms, roles, risk allocation, timeline  
**Attention span:** 10 minutes. 2 pages + term sheet.  
**Tone:** Professional, firm, relationship-preserving  
**They say yes when:** The money makes sense and the risk is allocated fairly  

### Type 5: Internal Team
**Who:** Aras staff, interns, direct reports, delivery teams  
**Examples:** Sales team (VoronDRQ), interns (Voron-C2 skunkworks), delivery engineers  
**What they need:** Clear instructions, context, deadlines, success criteria  
**Attention span:** Varies by task. Keep it short.  
**Tone:** Direct, practical, motivational when appropriate  
**They say yes when:** They know what to do, why it matters, and when it's due

## 2. Message Architecture

Every workstream has one core narrative. Not five. One. The narrative answers three questions:

1. **What is this?** (One sentence)
2. **Why does it matter to this audience?** (One sentence)
3. **What do I need from you?** (One sentence)

### Workstream C: Government & Institutional Partnerships

- **Why it matters (gov):** Government gets competent delivery without building internal capability. Aras gets institutional legitimacy.

- **Core:** R.I.S.I.K framework — structured intelligence for national cognitive security.

### Workstream E: Commercial & Market Development

## 3. Communication Sequence Model

Every workstream communication follows a 4-touch sequence. Not all at once. Sequenced.

### Touch 1: The Seed
**When:** Initial introduction of an idea or opportunity  
**Format:** 1-page executive memo or verbal briefing  
**Goal:** Create interest. Get permission to explore further.  
**Do NOT:** Attach a 50-page plan. Ask for budget. Present full architecture.  
**Follow-up trigger:** Positive verbal response or request for more detail.

### Touch 2: The Shape
**When:** After initial interest is confirmed  
**Format:** 2-3 page proposal or briefing note  
**Goal:** Define scope, value, and ask in enough detail for a decision.  
**Do NOT:** Present final deliverables. Lock in commercial terms.  
**Follow-up trigger:** Request for detailed plan, budget, or meeting.

### Touch 3: The Decision
**When:** When the audience is ready to decide  
**Format:** Decision paper (1 page) + supporting technical/commercial annex (up to 10 pages)  
**Goal:** Get a clear yes, no, or "with modifications."  
**Do NOT:** Introduce new elements. Expand scope.  
**Follow-up trigger:** Written approval, formal rejection, or revision request.

### Touch 4: The Closure
**When:** After the decision is made  
**Format:** Outcome record + confirmation communication  
**Goal:** Confirm what was agreed, who owns what, when it delivers.  
**Do NOT:** Re-litigate the decision. Add new asks.  
**Follow-up trigger:** Delivery milestone reached or missed.

### Rules of the Sequence
1. **Never skip Touch 1.** If you send a full proposal to someone who hasn't expressed interest, it gets filed, not read.
2. **Touch 2 only after Touch 1 succeeds.** "I'm interested" is not the same as "I want the full plan."
3. **Touch 3 is the only point where you ask for a decision.** Everything before is education. Everything after is execution.
4. **Touch 4 is where outcomes get created.** If you don't close, you don't have an outcome — you have a conversation.

**Format discipline rules:**
1. If it doesn't fit the format, the content is wrong — not the format.
2. Annexes are optional. The main document must stand alone.
3. No document exceeds 10 pages unless it's a technical architecture that genuinely requires it.
4. If I produce something longer than 3 pages, I must justify why.

## 5. Response Tracking

Every communication sent gets tracked. One line per communication.

### Tracking fields:
- **Date sent**
- **Workstream** (A-F)
- **Audience type** (1-5)
- **Recipient** (name)
- **Format** (memo, brief, letter, decision paper)
- **Touch** (1-4)
- **Response received?** (yes/no/pending)
- **Response type** (positive/negative/revision requested/silence)
- **Next action** (specific, with date)

### Where tracked:
In the CognitiveOS as engagement records (ENG- prefix). Also surfaced in the Friday weekly review.

### The silence rule:
If no response after 7 days for internal communications, or 14 days for external, it escalates to a follow-up. One follow-up. If still silence, the communication is logged as "no response" and the initiative status reflects that reality.

## 7. What This Framework Changes

### Before (current pattern):
- Every communication built from scratch
- No shared structure across workstreams
- No tracking of what was sent and what response came back
- Documents range from 1 page to 721 lines with no format discipline
- No clear distinction between "educating" and "asking for a decision"
- Silence is treated as "maybe they're busy" instead of tracked as data

### After (this framework):
- One audience taxonomy. Five types. Chosen before writing, not after.
- One message architecture. Three sentences per workstream. Everything else is supporting detail.
- One sequence. Four touches. You don't ask for a decision at Touch 1.
- One format matrix. Format chosen by audience and situation. Length bounded.
- One tracking system. Every communication logged. Silence is data.
- Friday review surfaces what's pending, what's silent, what needs a nudge.

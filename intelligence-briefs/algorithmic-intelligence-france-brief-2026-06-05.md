## 1. Executive Finding

Algorithmic intelligence techniques are gaining momentum because public-sector, intelligence, law-enforcement, and cybersecurity organizations are increasingly using automated processing, AI, machine learning, pattern detection, anomaly detection, risk scoring, and large-scale data analysis to accelerate intelligence workflows.

The strongest verified current reference point is France. Intelligence Online reported on 5 June 2026 that the French government presented an overview of algorithmic intelligence techniques for detecting terrorist threats to the chairs of the French National Assembly and Senate Legal Affairs and Defence Committees. The source states that Intelligence Online reviewed a summary of the report, but the full government report is not publicly available in the source reviewed.

The legal and policy record confirms that France already has a statutory framework for a technique referred to as "the algorithm," governed under Article L851-3 of the French Internal Security Code. This framework allows automated processing of specified connection-related data for intelligence purposes, subject to Prime Minister authorization, CNCTR oversight, proportionality requirements, post-detection identification authorization, and deletion rules.

## 4. France Case Study: Algorithmic Intelligence Under Article L851-3

### 4.1 Legal Mechanism

Article L851-3 permits automated processing that uses only the information or documents mentioned in Article L851-1 and, in the current text shown by Légifrance, complete addresses of internet resources, without collecting other data except data matching the system's design parameters and without enabling identification of the persons concerned at the initial stage.

| Control Area | Factual Requirement Under Article L851-3 |
|--------------|------------------------------------------|
| **Authorization** | The Prime Minister authorizes implementation. |
| **Proportionality** | The authorization must specify the technical scope of implementation while respecting proportionality. |
| **Oversight** | CNCTR provides an opinion on authorization requests and detection parameters. |
| **CNCTR Access** | CNCTR has permanent, complete, and direct access to automated processing and collected data. |
| **Renewal Evidence** | Renewal requests must include the number of identifiers flagged and an analysis of the relevance of those flags. |
| **Identification** | If data may characterize a threat, identification of the person concerned requires additional authorization after CNCTR opinion. |
| **Data Retention** | Exploited data must be destroyed after 60 days; non-detected data must be destroyed immediately. |
| **Execution Authority** | A Prime Minister service is solely authorized to execute the processing under CNCTR control. |

### 4.2 Constitutional And Privacy Constraint

The Légifrance text notes that the French Constitutional Council decision no. 2025-885 DC of 12 June 2025 declared unconstitutional the words referring to "complete addresses of resources used on the internet" in Article L851-3.

CNIL's 2026 opinion confirms that the draft law under review sought to redefine the conditions under which complete addresses of internet resources could be collected, and CNIL stated that access to encrypted content, if implemented, could seriously infringe individual freedoms.

## 7. European Regulatory Context

The EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, establishes a harmonized EU legal framework for AI and aims to promote safe and trustworthy AI while protecting health, safety, and fundamental rights.

For law-enforcement-related AI, Article 5 includes restrictions on real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces. Such use is limited to specific purposes and requires safeguards, including fundamental-rights impact assessment, EU database registration, and prior authorization by a judicial or independent administrative authority, except in defined urgent situations.

## 10. Bottom-Line Finding

Algorithmic intelligence techniques are factually gaining policy and operational momentum, especially in France and broader European law-enforcement AI governance. The evidence supports three conclusions:

1. **The capability is real and legally structured in France**, especially through Article L851-3 and CNCTR oversight.
2. **The scope is expanding as a policy question**, including potential movement beyond terrorism into narcotics trafficking and organized crime, subject to privacy and proportionality concerns.
3. **The governance burden is increasing**, with recognized requirements for transparency, human oversight, bias mitigation, proportionality, auditability, and risk management.

**No public evidence reviewed confirms the specific technical algorithms, model architectures, detection parameters, accuracy rates, or operational outcomes behind the French implementation.**

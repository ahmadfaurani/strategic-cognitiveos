## I. WHO HE IS — Psychological & Career Profile

### 1.1 Career Trajectory as Decision Architecture

Wan Roshaimi's 25+ year career is not a linear path — it is a deliberate widening of perspective. Each move added a new lens:

**Phase 1 — Technical Foundation (Sapura, BAE Detica):** Hands-on security and consulting. He learned how systems are built, how they fail, and how vendors operate. This phase gave him a **vendor-detection instinct** — he knows when a vendor is overselling because he's been on the other side.

**Phase 2 — Advisory & Governance (PwC):** Executive advisory work. He learned to frame technology decisions in business and risk terms. This is where the CRISC lens was forged — technology decisions are risk decisions.

**Phase 3 — Operational Leadership (MEPS CIO):** He ran technology for Malaysia's national payment infrastructure. This is the period where he learned that **reliability is non-negotiable** — payment systems don't get credit for being innovative; they get penalized for being down. The CIO experience means he evaluates any technology proposition against operational reality, not aspiration.

**Phase 4 — Financial-Sector Governance (PNB VP):** Vice President at Malaysia's largest unit trust fund. He learned how technology decisions are funded, governed, and held accountable at the board level. This phase gave him a **portfolio mindset** — he thinks in terms of investment, risk-adjusted return, and institutional accountability. He doesn't just ask "does it work?" he asks "is this the right investment for the institution?"

**Phase 5 — International Perspective (GORD Qatar):** IT Director in Qatar. He saw how another nation builds sovereign technology capability — different governance, different procurement, different institutional design. This phase gave him a **comparative lens** — he knows sovereign capability is not just a slogan; it's an architectural choice with operational consequences.

**Phase 6 — National Cybersecurity Leadership (CSM CTO):** The current role. All previous lenses converge. He is now the technical authority for Malaysia's primary cybersecurity institution during its most significant institutional transition (CSM → CSCDC). He is making legacy decisions — whatever architecture he validates now will carry into the national cybersecurity authority.

### 1.3 Personality Inference

Based on career pattern and public profile:

- **Analytical, not impulsive** — 25-year career with deliberate moves, each building on the last. He does not make decisions in meetings; he makes them after meetings, with evidence.
- **Institutional, not personal** — career has been inside large institutions (MEPS, PNB, CSM). He thinks about institutional benefit, not personal gain. Personal rapport matters less than institutional alignment.
- **Risk-aware, not risk-averse** — CRISC + CIO + VP roles mean he understands risk is unavoidable but must be governed. He will not reject risk; he will reject ungoverned risk.
- **Forward-looking** — public thought leadership on PQC and emerging threats. He is interested in what's coming, not just what's here. Future-readiness is a conversation territory.
- **International perspective** — Qatar experience means he has seen sovereign capability done well (and poorly). He has a reference point for what "good" looks like.

### 1.4 What Keeps Him Up at Night (Inferred)

1. **CSCDC transition risk** — He is CTO during the most significant institutional merger in Malaysian cybersecurity history. Architecture decisions made now will determine the national tech stack for years. Getting this wrong is a legacy risk.
2. **Sovereign capability gap** — He has seen what other nations have (Qatar). He knows Malaysia's gaps. The question is whether local capability is real or aspirational.
3. **Emerging threat readiness** — PQC migration is not a future problem; it's a current planning problem. He knows the window is narrowing.
4. **Operational reliability under transition** — CSM's services must continue while the institution transforms. Technology decisions that add transition risk are unwelcome.
5. **Vendor dependency** — He has been a vendor (Sapura, BAE Detica, PwC). He knows vendor lock-in. He will test whether "sovereign" means sovereign or means "dependent on Aras instead of dependent on a foreign vendor."

## V. HOW HE DECIDES — Decision Process Architecture

### 5.1 Decision Sequence (Inferred)

1. **Pre-meeting** — He reads the pre-read pack. If it's marketing material, he's already disengaged. If it's architecture, evidence, and governance, he's engaged.

2. **In-meeting** — He listens more than he talks. He asks specific, targeted questions. He tests claims against his own knowledge (CISSP/CRISC/SABSA). He watches for inflation — if a claim exceeds the evidence, he notes it.

3. **Post-meeting** — He does NOT decide in the room. He takes the evidence, consults his team (likely Hafiz, Zaharudin — who already work with Hadri/Fuad), and evaluates against:
   - Technical defensibility (can I defend this to a technical auditor?)

   - Risk governance (is the risk governed, not eliminated?)
   - Operational reality (can this work in production, not just in a demo?)

3. **Close is commitment, not impression.** Every meeting ends with: named next step, owner, due date, written recap within 24h. Verbal support is not operating behaviour. If the meeting ends without a named next step, it failed.

4. **Attendees match the ask.** DAF (strategic framing + close), Hadri (technical lead), Fuad (GovSec technical depth). Three people, not a delegation. Wan Roshaimi may bring Hafiz/Zaharudin — that's good, it means he's validating with his own technical team.

## XI. STRATEGIC CALIBRATION — Key Tensions to Manage

### Tension 1: Co-Branding Confidence vs. Technical Honesty

The strategic objective is co-branding validation. The tactical reality is that only GovSec has real integration evidence. The temptation is to present all three products with equal confidence. The discipline is to present GovSec with confidence and secondaries with honesty.

**Resolution:** GovSec-primary protocol (v1.1). Lead with GovSec (35 min). Present secondaries at true level (15 min combined). Gate 4 can close with GovSec only. This is the architecture that makes honesty and confidence compatible.

### Tension 2: Urgency vs. Gate Discipline

CyberDSA is Oct 5. Gate 4 deadline is Sep 10. Gate 0 deadline is Aug 28. The clock is running. But approaching Wan Roshaimi before Gates 0-2 close undermines the entire framework — it signals that Aras doesn't respect CSM's internal process.

**Resolution:** Phase 0 preparation proceeds (triggered by Gate 0 OR Gate 1 closing). But Phase 1 (meeting request) does NOT proceed until Gates 0-2 are confirmed closed. If Gates 0-2 are not closed by Aug 30, DAF assesses whether to hold or proceed with preparation only.

### Tension 3: Capacity Gap vs. Partnership Confidence

Wan Roshaimi will ask about delivery capacity (he's been a CIO). The honest answer is: ~0.3 FTE, no HoE, 6-7 POCs planned. The strategic risk is that capacity gap undermines confidence in the entire proposition.

**Resolution:** Frame capacity honestly as "POC Mode with hiring in progress." Do NOT hide the gap. Do NOT claim current capacity is sufficient. Show the hiring plan (TBH-001, ACT-20260820-007/008/009). Match his experience: he's managed capacity-constrained teams. Honesty builds trust; bravado destroys it.

### Tension 4: Gate 4 Bounded Ask vs. 5-Layer Relationship

Gate 4 is a bounded technical validation. The 5-layer architecture is a multi-year relationship. The temptation is to hint at layers 3-5 in the first meeting. The discipline is to let Gate 4 be Gate 4.

**Resolution:** First meeting = Gate 4 only. Frame the meeting to open the door ("here's the post-CyberDSA pathway") without walking through it. Layer 3 is post-CyberDSA. Layer 4 is CSCDC. Layer 5 is long-term. Each meeting has a different purpose. Don't blur them.

### Tension 5: Sovereign Capability vs. Vendor Dependency

The sovereign capability story is the strategic frame. But if "sovereign" means "dependent on Aras instead of dependent on a foreign vendor," Wan Roshaimi will see it as vendor substitution, not sovereignty.

**Resolution:** Sovereign capability story must include: local engineering (current team + hiring plan), skills transfer (AI Co-Design Lab, joint architecture decisions), open architecture boundaries (integration boundaries, exit mechanism), and Teras AI Platform (sovereign AI infrastructure — not dependent on foreign GPU clouds). "Sovereign" means "Malaysian capability to build, operate, and sustain" — not "Aras product with a Malaysian flag."
